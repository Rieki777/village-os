# Sociocracy / Consent-Based Governance

> Sociocracy (also called dynamic governance; the classic form is Endenburg's Sociocratic Circle-organization Method, SCM) is a governance system that distributes authority into small linked teams ("circles") and makes decisions by consent (no reasoned objection) instead of majority vote or full-group consensus. A founder reaches for it when whole-group meetings have become slow or painful, when a few voices dominate (or block), or when the community is growing past the size where everyone can decide everything together. It is the fastest-spreading formal governance system in cohousing and ecovillages today. Full-group consensus remains the historical default, but dozens of communities have switched (Cohousing Association of the US). Key orgs: Sociocracy For All (SoFA), The Sociocracy Group (TSG, Endenburg lineage), and the Sociocracy 3.0 (S3) project.

## Core patterns

**1. Consent, not consensus.** A decision passes when nobody has a *reasoned objection*, not when everyone loves it. The test question is "is this good enough for now, safe enough to try?", not "is this your preference?" An objection must argue the proposal would harm the circle's aim or capacity to work; a preference does not block. Consensus asks for agreement; consent asks for absence of argued disagreement: a lower, faster threshold that still protects everyone from harm. Abstaining/standing aside is not allowed: you consent or you object, so hesitations surface instead of hiding. (Sociocracy For All, "Sociocracy: basic concepts"; SoFA, "Consensus vs. dynamic governance".)
*Contested:* veteran consensus facilitator Laird Schaub argues the consent/consensus distinction is largely semantic; well-run consensus already uses a "good of the group" test. SoFA and Diana Leafe Christian counter that in practice the explicit objection standard plus circle delegation changes behavior dramatically. Present both to communities coming from consensus culture.

**2. Circles with real authority over a defined domain.** Each circle (ideally 4-8 people) has a written *aim* (what it does) and *domain* (what it alone decides). Gardeners decide garden policy; the finance circle decides bookkeeping policy, without plenary ratification. This is the big structural difference from consensus communities, where committees only draft proposals for the whole group. Delegation is what makes meetings shorter and burnout lower. (SoFA; Rau & Koch-Gonzalez, *Many Voices One Song*.)

**3. Double-linking.** A parent circle and sub-circle are connected by two people: the *leader* (appointed top-down by the parent circle) and the *delegate* (elected bottom-up by the sub-circle). Both sit as full consenting members of the parent circle. Why: one link carries direction down, the other carries feedback up, so information can't be bottlenecked by a single person and sub-circles can't be steamrolled. (Endenburg SCM via sociocracy.info; SoFA.)

**4. Role elections by consent (open nominations).** Roles (facilitator, leader, delegate, secretary/logkeeper) are filled by a structured process: (1) review the role description and term; (2) nomination round: each person nominates someone *with reasons tied to the role description*; (3) change round after hearing others' reasons; (4) facilitator proposes a candidate based on the strength of reasons, not vote counts; (5) consent round, integrating any objections. No self-put-forward campaigning, no secret ballots. Why: roles go to people who have the group's explicit trust, and the reasons said aloud build that trust. Always set a term (1-2 years typical). (SoFA, "Selection process"; sociocracy.info, "The Sociocratic Election Process".)

**5. Rounds.** The signature meeting tool: speak one-by-one until everyone has spoken, no interruptions, no crosstalk. Used for check-ins, reactions, consent, evaluations. Why: equalizes airtime, slows reactivity, makes the quiet people's information available. (Ted Rau, "On rounds".)

**6. Standard meeting format.** Check-in round → consent to the agenda → content items (reports, explorations, decisions) → meeting evaluation round. For a decision item: present proposal → clarifying-questions round (understanding only) → quick-reactions round (brief opinions, "I will object because…") → consent round → integrate objections. Meetings are typically 60-120 minutes, facilitated, with minutes logged. (SoFA, "The sociocratic meeting format".)

**7. Objections are information, and every policy has a review date.** Standard integration moves: amend the proposal, shorten the term or add a measurement ("try for 6 months, then review"), narrow its scope, or test whether the objection actually relates to the aim. Every policy gets an expiry/review date (nothing is decided forever), which lowers the stakes of consenting and keeps agreements alive instead of fossilizing. (SoFA; *Many Voices One Song*.)

**8. Feedback loops everywhere.** Meeting evaluations at every meeting, periodic role reviews (peer feedback for role-holders), and scheduled policy reviews. Why: the system self-corrects through small frequent feedback instead of crisis-driven overhauls. (SoFA.)

**9. Classic sociocracy (SCM) vs. Sociocracy 3.0 (S3).** SCM is a coherent whole method: circles, double links, consent, elections; you adopt the system. S3 (Priest, Bockelbrink & David) is a Creative-Commons pattern library of 65+ patterns organized around "drivers" (the tension/need behind any action); you adopt patterns piecemeal as needed. S3 suits organizations that want to evolve gradually; SCM/SoFA-style suits communities that want one complete, teachable system. Most intentional communities use the SCM/SoFA lineage; S3 is more common in companies and networks. (sociocracy30.org, "Practical Guide"; SoFA.)

## In practice

**Typical village structure:** a General Circle (coordinates, holds budget and cross-domain policy; composed of leaders + delegates of the sub-circles) with 3-6 functional circles, commonly: Land & Buildings, Membership, Finance/Legal, Community Life / Process. The full-community plenary is kept, but repurposed: connection, reports, feedback, and only the biggest decisions (mission, annual budget, membership criteria, selling land). Everything else lives in circles. (SoFA; Diana Leafe Christian, "Sociocracy for Communities".)

**First steps (3-6 months):**
1. Get 2-5 people trained (SoFA runs low-cost cohort courses; *Many Voices One Song* is the standard manual; *We the People* by Buck & Villines is the SCM classic).
2. Form an implementation/governance circle with an explicit mandate to draft the circle structure, domains, and initial policies.
3. Write domains down. Vague domains are the #1 source of later turf conflict.
4. Adopt formally: a plenary decision (by whatever your current method is) that the community is switching, with a built-in review at 12 months. Stealth or informal adoption gets undone. (SoFA, "Common pitfalls".)
5. Elect facilitators and delegates by the consent election process; train more than one facilitator per circle and rotate.
6. Start a decision log / policy register from day one: every policy, its date, and its review date. Communities that skip this re-litigate everything.

**Cadence:** circles meet every 2-4 weeks; General Circle monthly; plenary monthly-to-quarterly. Consent decisions on well-prepared proposals typically take 15-30 minutes.

**Tools:** written role descriptions with terms; a shared policy register (wiki, doc, or your platform); proposal templates ("background / proposal / term / review date"); meeting minutes visible to all members. Transparency of records is part of the system, not an add-on.

## Failure modes

- **The General Circle hoards power / sub-circles punt everything back up.** Early sign: agenda items keep escalating "just to check." Fix: restate domains in writing; the parent circle answers help requests but refuses to decide what it delegated. (SoFA, "Common pitfalls".)
- **Vocabulary without training.** The group says "consent" and "circle" but runs the same old meeting. Consent degrades into either a veto culture (preferences dressed as objections) or rubber-stamping (nobody objects out of politeness). Fix: facilitator openly tests objections against the aim ("how would this harm our work?"), and invests in real training. SoFA is blunt that goodwill alone does not change governance behavior.
- **Using governance change to fix a relational rift.** SoFA's own warning: implementing a new system on top of deep interpersonal conflict "is likely going to make things even worse." Sociocracy has no built-in emotional processing (Schaub's strongest critique). Practitioner response: pair it with explicit conflict/communication practice (NVC, restorative circles) and don't schedule structural change during an active conflict.
- **Volunteer overload.** Double-linking multiplies roles; small communities chronically can't fill them (Schaub). Signs: same three people hold every role; delegates skip parent-circle meetings. Fixes: fewer circles (merge domains), combine roles, shorten meetings, accept a single link temporarily and name the tradeoff.
- **Elections that feel unsafe.** Voicing reservations about a neighbor in real time is hard. Mitigation practitioners use: keep all reasons anchored to the written role description ("it's nothing personal," Sociocracy Consulting Group), never assume someone's willingness, and let candidates object for themselves.
- **Formality rejection.** Some communities experience the machinery (roles, logs, rounds, review dates) as heavy and abandon or heavily adapt it within a few years (Damien Douté's critique of sociocracy in cohousing). Early sign: attendance drops, "can't we just talk?" Fix: shrink the apparatus to match group size while keeping consent itself; a 15-person community may need only two circles.
- **No review dates / no decision log.** Policies fossilize, newcomers inherit rules nobody remembers deciding, and old fights reopen. This is the cheapest failure to prevent.

## When NOT to use this

- **Very small groups (under ~6-8 active adults):** run one circle with consent decision-making and skip the multi-circle structure and double links. The full apparatus is overhead.
- **Acute conflict:** stabilize relationships first (mediation, facilitated dialogue); governance redesign mid-conflict tends to become another battlefield.
- **No appetite for training or facilitation:** without at least a few trained facilitators the format decays fast; a lighter method honestly applied beats sociocracy badly applied.
- **A consensus culture that is genuinely working:** switching costs are real. Alternatives practitioners recommend: the N St. Consensus Method (consensus-minus-blocking safeguards); Diana Leafe Christian suggests it for communities not ready for sociocracy.
- **Short-lived or single-project groups:** the payoff comes from iterated feedback loops; a group dissolving in six months won't collect it.
- **Legal-structure caveat:** if your HOA, co-op, or nonprofit bylaws mandate member voting, sociocracy operates inside or alongside those requirements: bylaws and consent process must be reconciled (many communities encode "decisions by consent, fallback vote" in bylaws). This is orientation, not legal advice; rules vary by jurisdiction. Consult a lawyer familiar with your entity type.
